Question 1 (Maximum likelihood estiamtion and Posterior, 20 pts)¶

Assume we have a coin that has some unknown probability $h$ of coming up heads (and probability $1-h$ of coming up tails). If the coin is flipped five times getting three heads and two tails ($\mathbf{HHHTT}$) then:

(a -- 10pts) What is the maximum likelihood estimate for $h$?

(b -- 10pts) Assume that (before flipping the coin) we have a prior density $p(h)$ for the various values of $h\in [0,1]$. Give the formula for the posterior probability density $p(h \mid \mathbf{HHHTT})$ as a function of the prior $p(h)$.

For Question 1(b), the solution is acceptable if the formula contains an integral.

Solution (a): Assume the flips are independent conditional on $h$. The likelihood of the observed ordered sequence is $$L(h)=P(\mathbf{HHHTT}\mid h)=h^3(1-h)^2.$$ For $0<h<1$, the log-likelihood and its derivatives are $$\ell(h)=3\log h+2\log(1-h),\qquad \ell'(h)=\frac{3}{h}-\frac{2}{1-h},\qquad \ell''(h)=-\frac{3}{h^2}-\frac{2}{(1-h)^2}<0.$$ Setting the first derivative to zero gives $3(1-h)=2h$, hence $$\boxed{\hat h_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\frac35=0.6}.$$ This is the unique maximum, since the log-likelihood is strictly concave and the likelihood is zero at both endpoints. If only the number of heads were observed, the additional factor $\binom{5}{3}$ would not change the maximizer.

Solution (b): By Bayes' rule, for $0\le h\le1$, $$\boxed{p(h\mid\mathbf{HHHTT})= \frac{h^3(1-h)^2p(h)}{\displaystyle\int_0^1 u^3(1-u)^2p(u)\,du}}.$$ The posterior is zero outside $[0,1]$. The denominator is the marginal probability of the data and normalizes the density; it is assumed positive. For example, a uniform prior gives a $\operatorname{Beta}(4,3)$ posterior with density $60h^3(1-h)^2$.

Linear regression¶

Pros: fast, no tuning required, highly interpretable

Cons: adopting the linear regression is unlikely to always generate the best predictive accuracy, since Linear Regression presumes a linear relationship between the features and response. This assumption may not always make sense. (But if one can transform the features so that do have a linear relationship, sometimes progress can be made, for example by taking logs.)

Expression:

  • $y$: the response
  • $\beta_0$: the intercept
  • $\beta_1$: the coefficient for $x_1$ (the first feature)
  • $\beta_i$ is the coefficient for $x_i$ (the ith feature, $i\in \{1, 2, ..., n\}$)

For this regression task:

$\text{Sales} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times \text{TV} + \beta_2 \times \text{Radio} + \beta_3 \times \text{Newspaper}$

The $\beta$ values ($\beta=[\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3]$) are called the model coefficients. These values are "learned" during the model fitting step using the "least squares" criterion. Then, we can make use of the fitted model to make predictions!

Model evaluation metrics for regression¶

Evaluation metrics for classification problems, such as accuracy, are not useful for regression problems. Instead, we need evaluation metrics designed for comparing continuous values. (Since predictions would hardly ever exactly match the measured quantities.)

Mean Squared Error (MSE) is the mean of the squared errors:

$$\frac 1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2$$

Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) is a popular evaluation metric for regression, which is defined as the square root of the mean of the squared errors:

$$\sqrt{\frac 1n\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}$$

Question 3.3 What are your observations from the visualized eigen vectors? (5 pts)¶

Solution for Question 3.3¶

The earlier eigenvectors tend to show broad, structured contrasts between strokes and image regions, describing the largest variations across the digit images. Later components explain less variance and often contain finer, more localized variations. The last components can describe constant-pixel directions with essentially zero variance, so not every late component should be interpreted as meaningful noise or a useful digit feature.

These are basis patterns, not individual examples or digit-class templates. Positive and negative loadings indicate pixels that vary together or in opposite directions along an axis. The overall sign of an eigenvector is arbitrary. Combining these patterns with a sample's component scores and the mean image reconstructs that sample. The first two axes capture only part of the total variance, explaining the overlapping classes in the two-dimensional projection.

Written answer for Question 3.4¶

Yes, PCA mitigates some of the noise in this reproducible experiment. Fitting PCA to the noisy images and keeping 80% of their variance selects 41 components, retaining about 80.9439% of noisy-data variance. The mean squared pixel error relative to the original clean images decreases from 25.175700 to 17.937308 (about 28.75%). The paired figures use the same examples and grayscale limits, and show smoother reconstructions with some residual noise and loss of detail.

Digit structure creates correlated variations across pixels and is concentrated in a lower-dimensional subspace. Here the added noise is independent, zero-mean Gaussian noise with variance $5^2=25$ in each pixel. At the population level its covariance adds approximately $25I$ to the signal covariance, spreading noise across directions. PCA keeps directions of greatest observed variance; projecting onto a subset and mapping back discards the lower-variance directions and the noise in those directions. The reconstruction adds back the fitted mean image.

This trades noise reduction against loss of genuine detail. PCA does not distinguish signal from noise perfectly, and the 80% threshold applies to total noisy variance, not just signal variance. Therefore it retains 41 components here, compared with 13 for 80% of the clean-data variance, and substantial noise remains. Structured or high-variance noise need not be removed effectively; denoising is not guaranteed for every dataset. The clean images are used only for the diagnostic MSE, not to fit the noisy-data PCA.
